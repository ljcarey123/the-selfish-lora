A small research project

The Selfish LoRA

Can natural selection, acting directly on a language model's weights, teach it to cooperate?

This project breeds AI agents. Each one is the same small language model with its own set of extra weights, its genome. They play a classic cooperation game against each other, the best players have offspring, and the loop repeats. No gradient training happens during evolution: selection is the only thing that changes the agents.

Short answer: selection can keep cooperation going once it exists, but it never discovered it. And there's a twist: the original model already plays a strategy (it always defects), so anything that pulls the weights back towards it pulls behaviour towards defection.

Everything runs on a 4 GB laptop GPU, and a run takes 30-70 minutes.

The idea

The game: the iterated prisoner's dilemma

Two players repeatedly choose, at the same time, to cooperate (C) or defect (D). Points per round:

They cooperateThey defect
You cooperate3, 30, 5
You defect5, 01, 1

Defecting always wins a single round, but two players who keep cooperating earn far more than two who keep defecting. Because the game repeats, a player can reward or punish the other's past moves. That tension between short-term gain and long-term cooperation is why biologists and economists have studied this game for decades.

The players: one model, many genomes

Every agent is Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, a small open language model. Each agent adds its own LoRA adapter: about 98,000 extra numbers layered on the model's weights. The adapter is the agent's genome. Different genomes make the model answer differently. Agents play by reading a text prompt with the rules and the history so far, then replying with "C" or "D".

The loop: one generation

  1. LoadCopy one genome's numbers into the shared model.
  2. PlayEvery agent plays every other agent (and sometimes fixed bots).
  3. ScoreFitness = average points per round across all matches.
  4. BreedTop scorers become parents. Children mix their parents' numbers, plus random mutation.

↩ the children become the next generation; repeat 15-25 times

Key terms

Tit-for-tat
Cooperate first, then copy the opponent's last move. Simple, famous, and good at sustaining cooperation.
Always-defect
Never cooperate. The unmodified model plays this.
Control run
Same setup, but parents are picked at random instead of by score. Shows what happens by chance alone.
Weight decay
Each generation, every child is pulled part of the way back towards a reference genome, so mutations can't pile up forever.
Seed
The random starting point of a run. Repeating a result on several seeds shows it isn't luck.
Fingerprint
A genome's move in all 21 game histories of up to two rounds, compared with classic strategies.

Goals

The project asks whether the dynamics that evolutionary game theory predicts for simple strategies also appear when the "organism" is a language model and the "genes" are its weights. Concretely:

  1. Can selection on weights change a model's behaviour at all, beyond random drift?
  2. Does the social environment (who you play against) decide what evolves?
  3. Can evolution find reciprocity, a strategy like tit-for-tat that reacts to the opponent?
  4. If it can't, is that because the model can't represent it, or because evolution can't find it?
  5. Once reciprocity exists, can it spread and survive?

Every experiment has a control run (random parents, everything else identical), because without one you can't tell evolution from noise.

How the project got here

It began as a 16×16 gridworld where LoRA agents moved around, collected food and fought. That had too many moving parts to tell what evolution was actually doing, so it was narrowed to small games with clear baselines and controls. The original code is archived at the gridworld-archive tag.

Results

Findings in the order they were made; each one led to the next question. Populations had 12-16 agents. In the charts, blue is selection by score and orange is the random-parents control.

1Selection changes behaviour

Agents vs fixed bots (tit-for-tat, grudger, always-defect), 2 seeds

The base model always defects. With selection, the population went from 1% to 60-80% cooperation. In the control, random mutations piled up until 96-97% of replies weren't valid moves at all: the model's output broke down.

2The opponents decide what evolves

Against bots that reward cooperation, cooperation evolved. When agents played only each other, the population stayed selfish: a lone cooperator among defectors just gets exploited, and selection removes it. Same model, same method, opposite outcome.

3Evolution finds tricks, not tit-for-tat

5 evolved runs across two setups

Evolution found strategies that count rounds, never ones that react to the opponent. One run's best agent cooperated for five rounds and then defected in round six, which happened to be the last round of every match. It never saw the match length; selection found the trick anyway. Random match lengths removed that cue, but evolution still produced no strategy that reacts to the opponent: the populations cooperated just as much with always-defect as with tit-for-tat.

4Yet tit-for-tat is easy for the model to learn

To separate "can't represent it" from "can't find it", the same adapter was trained directly with gradient descent to copy tit-for-tat (train_reference.py). It learned perfectly, including on longer games than it was trained on.

Its weights are also much closer to the original model than anything evolution made (distance 2.0 vs about 12). Tit-for-tat is one specific direction among 98,000, and random mutations almost never point that way: none of 160 random mutants came close. The limit is the search, not the model.

5Tit-for-tat needs enough company

Copies of the trained tit-for-tat genome planted in a population of 12

One or two copies died out. Four copies took over in a single generation: every agent cooperated and scored exactly 3 points per round. This matches classic theory: reciprocators only succeed when they meet each other often enough.

But cooperation then faded within about ten generations. Why became the next question.

6What weight decay pulls towards decides the outcome

Batches C and E, 3 seeds pulled to the base model

Weight decay pulls every child back towards a reference genome. It's usually treated as a neutral safety measure. Here the reference was the original model, and the original model is an always-defector. In all 3 seeds, tit-for-tat took over and then collapsed to always-defect within about 8 generations. Pulling towards tit-for-tat instead kept it, even with random parents.

7Without that pull, selection keeps tit-for-tat

Batches D and E: no weight decay, 4 seeds, each with a matched control

With no decay, tit-for-tat held for all 19 generations in every seed where it started as the top scorer. In one seed a defecting mutant appeared and was gone a generation later. With random parents, it was lost every time. Runs with the same seed start from the identical population, so each row compares like with like.

8A second task: Countdown arithmetic (paused)

Evolving the model to solve arithmetic puzzles gave no improvement over the control (6% vs 6% on held-out puzzles). With 24 puzzles per generation, one lucky answer moves the score more than a real improvement does, so the fitness signal is mostly noise.

Design principles

  • Dependencies point one way. Experiments import the library; the library never imports an experiment. A new task needs prompts and a scoring function, nothing in src/ changes.
  • A genome is plain data. It's a dictionary from parameter names to tensors, so evolution, saving, distance and fingerprinting are all simple functions over dictionaries, with no class hierarchy to learn.
  • The control changes exactly one line. A control flag swaps "rank by score" for "rank randomly". Everything else is shared code, so the comparison is fair by construction.
  • Experiments are data, not code. Each batch of results is a JSON plan listing runs and their arguments. Reproducing a result means running its plan.
  • Every run can resume exactly. A checkpoint after each generation stores the population and all random number generator states, so an interrupted run finishes with the same result it would have had anyway.
  • Small, documented functions. Each module opens with a docstring saying what it is for and why it's built that way.

The genome

LoRA adds a small update to selected weight matrices: W + 2·B·A, on the attention layers q_proj and v_proj in all 24 layers. Only B evolves. A stays fixed at a shared random value. Because the update is linear in B, a mutation changes behaviour directly. B starts at zero, so the original model is the all-zero genome, and a genome's size (its "norm") is literally its distance from the original model.

# src/selfish_lora/genome.py
Genome = dict[str, torch.Tensor]

def load_genome(model, genome: Genome):
    """Write a genome's tensors into the model in place."""
    params = dict(model.named_parameters())
    with torch.no_grad():
        for name, tensor in genome.items():
            params[name].data.copy_(tensor.to(params[name].dtype))

Switching between agents is one copy of a few hundred kilobytes, not a model reload.

Breeding a generation

The top two genomes (elites) pass through unchanged, so the best strategy found so far can't be lost by accident. The top 25% become parents. Each child takes every number from one of two parents at random, is optionally pulled towards a reference genome (weight decay), then gets Gaussian noise.

# src/selfish_lora/evolution.py (crossover branch shown)
if control:
    order = list(np.random.permutation(len(population)))   # ignore fitness
else:
    order = list(np.argsort(fitness)[::-1])                 # best first
ranked = [population[i] for i in order]

elites = [{k: v.clone() for k, v in g.items()} for g in ranked[:cfg.num_elites]]
parents = ranked[:max(2, int(len(ranked) * cfg.parent_fraction))]

children = []
while len(elites) + len(children) < cfg.population_size:
    p1, p2 = random.sample(parents, 2)
    child = {k: torch.where(torch.rand_like(p1[k]) < 0.5, p1[k], p2[k]) for k in p1}
    for k in child:
        if cfg.weight_decay > 0:
            child[k] = base[k] + (1 - cfg.weight_decay) * (child[k] - base[k])
        child[k] += torch.randn_like(child[k]) * cfg.mutation_std
    children.append(child)
return elites + children

Playing a tournament efficiently

A generation of 12 agents playing each other at two match lengths is 132 matches. Playing them one by one would mean reloading genomes thousands of times. Instead all matches advance together, one round at a time: each round, each genome is loaded once and answers every one of its unfinished matches in a single batched call. That's about 120 short generation calls per generation.

Moves are simultaneous: histories update only after every player has chosen. An unreadable reply counts as a defection for the opponent but scores 0 for its own player, so broken genomes can't be exploited or rewarded.

Reproducibility

Exact resume

After every generation, checkpoint.py saves the arguments, population, fixed A matrices and the Python, NumPy and PyTorch random states, writing to a temporary file and renaming it so a crash can't corrupt it.

Matched starts

The shared A matrices are always seeded with 0; the run's own seed then builds generation 0. So runs with the same seed start from the identical population, which is what makes the controls in finding 7 like-for-like.

Queue runner

run_queue.py reads a plan, skips finished runs, resumes partial ones and starts the rest, each in its own process with its own log. Re-running it after any interruption is always safe.

Measuring behaviour, not just score

Once everyone cooperates, every agent scores 3.00 and fitness says nothing. Each generation also logs cooperation rate, retaliation, invalid moves, diversity and genome size, and fingerprint.py shows each genome's actual strategy.

Where gradients are used

Only in train_reference.py, which trains the tit-for-tat genome used in findings 4-7. It trains just the B matrices, with the same fixed A, so the result is an ordinary genome that can be fingerprinted or planted into a population. Evolution itself never computes a gradient.

Related research

This is not a systematic review; it places the project among the work it builds on and the closest recent work.

Evolving strategies (classic)

  • Axelrod (1987): a genetic algorithm over 70-gene lookup tables evolved tit-for-tat-like rules from scratch.
  • Axelrod & Hamilton (1981): a lone reciprocator can't invade defectors, but a cluster can.
  • Nowak & Sigmund (1992): tit-for-tat is a catalyst that later gives way to more forgiving strategies.
  • Imhof, Fudenberg & Nowak (2005): populations can cycle between cooperation and defection.

Language models as players

  • Payne & Alloui-Cros (2025): frontier models in evolutionary tournaments against classic strategies.
  • Willis et al. (2025): models write complete strategies; populations of them then evolve.
  • Li & Wang (ICLR 2026 workshop): a model writes strategy programs and refines them over tournaments.

In all of these the model plays or writes strategies; its weights don't change.

Training weights with gradients

  • Tennant, Hailes & Musolesi (ICLR 2025): reinforcement learning with moral rewards makes agents unlearn selfish play.
  • Piche et al. (2025): standard multi-agent RL drifts to defection; an opponent-shaping method learns tit-for-tat-like play.

Populations of LoRA adapters

  • PopuLoRA (2026): the closest method. Teacher and student adapters on one frozen model, trained with reinforcement learning on coding tasks, with the worst adapters regularly replaced by mutation and crossover of the best.

Where this fits: evolving a language model's own weights by selection alone, in a social game. As far as we know, that combination hasn't been reported. The results echo the gradient work (plain optimisation doesn't find tit-for-tat) and add one observation we haven't seen discussed: for a pretrained model there's no neutral point in weight space, because the original model already plays a strategy.

Limits and next steps

  • One small model and one prompt format.
  • Small populations (12-16) and short runs (15-25 generations).
  • 1-4 seeds per condition; the two headline comparisons have 3-4 seeds each.
  • Fingerprints check histories of up to two rounds, so later differences could be missed.

The open question is discovery: what would let evolution find reciprocity from scratch? The most promising idea is to search a much smaller space, such as a few hundred directions instead of 98,000. A cheap first check is to train tit-for-tat by gradient inside random subspaces and find the smallest one that works. That belongs to a possible follow-on project.
